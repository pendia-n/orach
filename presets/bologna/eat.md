why is Bologna so famous for its food? And it has a lot to do with its location, climate, and traditions.\n\nThe city is the capital of Emilia-Romagna, the region responsible for a large share of Italy's greatest food, including Parmigiano Reggiano, prosciutto di Parma, traditional balsamic, Mortadella, tortellini, and Ragù. This is peasant food that got very, very good over generations.\n\nThe osterie fed factory workers and students, not aristocrats, and you can still feel that when you’re tucking into a bowl of tagliatelle in a proper Bolognese trattoria, and why it’s only €10 - affordable, nutritious, for everyone.\n\nBologna takes the tradition so seriously that it legislated it, and several of the city's core recipes have been formally deposited with the Bologna Chamber of Commerce.\n\nRecently, Bologna's best modern kitchens started interrogating the traditions, through better sourcing, less waste, different techniques, and a running question about what a Bolognese restaurant should actually look like in 2026.\n\nOne meal here might be served under framed family photographs by someone whose mother rolled pasta in the same room, and the next might involve natural wine and a Bolognese-inspired ramen, and we think this evolution is exciting.\n\nThe tension between guarding traditions and pushing boundaries is what makes eating in Bologna more interesting than eating almost anywhere else in Italy.\n\n![The_Common_Wanderer_-0546.jpg]()\n\n![The_Common_Wanderer_-2535.jpg]()\n\n### BOLOGNA'S ICONIC DISHES TO TRY\n\nDuring our multiple trips to Bologna, we may or may not have added a few kilos to our waistlines, and we're not sorry about it. Thin sheets of spinach pasta layered with ragù, béchamel and Parmigiano. No ricotta, no mozzarella, no boiled egg. The pasta is the point; the sauces bind rather than smother.\n\n**TORTELLONI BURRO E SALVIA** | larger and more loosely folded than tortellini, filled with ricotta and Parmigiano, dressed simply in butter and sage.\n\nOne of Bologna's great uncomplicated pleasures, and our standout dish at Trattoria del Rosso.\n\n**GRAMIGNA ALLA SALSICCIA** | short, tightly curled pasta with a rich sausage sauce. Rustic and peppery, where the beef ragù is deep and slow.\n\n**BALANZONI** | green spinach parcels filled with ricotta, mortadella and Parmigiano, named after Dr Balanzone, the pompous Bolognese physician of the commedia dell'arte, who supposedly ate them at Carnival.\n\nOnce you've worked through the famous four, this is what we'd order next.\n\n**MORTADELLA** | the one Bologna is arguably most famous for globally, and the most misunderstood. Real Mortadella Bologna IGP is finely ground pork studded with cubes of pork-jowl fat (*lardelli*), gently spiced and slow-cooked in large ovens for up to 24 hours. Delicate, aromatic, sliced almost translucent.\n\nWhipped into a *spuma di mortadella* mousse, it's a revelation.\n\n**COTOLETTA ALLA BOLOGNESE** | a breaded veal cutlet topped with prosciutto and Parmigiano, finished with broth until the cheese melts. Completely different from the supermarket version most people know. Giorgio’s family has been making vinegar for 130 years and the tradition is passed down to each generation—Giorgio is now working with his son-in-law to keep it alive.\n\nGiorgio’s English-speaking wife Giovanna explained the vast difference between supermarket balsamic vinegar and traditional DOP balsamic vinegar which is strictly regulated for quality by a consortium.\n\nThe real stuff is made from 100% grape must with nothing added—it is cooked at a low temperature for a long time until it reduces to thick black liquid gold. It can only be made in Modena.\n\nAt the Acetaia the vinegar is aged in small wooden barrels for at least 12 or 25 years.\n\nIt’s a long, painstaking process where some of the vinegar is moved from one barrel to another as it evaporates, year after year.\n\nFinally, we got to try the final product and it was like no vinegar we’d ever tasted, thick, sweet and intense.\n\n![The range of balsamic vinegars at Acetaia di Giorgio, Modena, Italy](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns%3D%22http%3A%2F%2Fwww.w3.org%2F2000%2Fsvg%22%20viewBox%3D%220%200%201100%20825%22%3E%3C%2Fsvg%3E)\n\nTraditional DOP balsamic vinegar must be sold in these small round bottles.\n\nThe type of wood that’s used for the barrels really makes a difference—the juniper was spicy and the cherry fruity.\n\nAnd the 25-year-old really does taste sweeter and richer than the 12-year-old—it doesn’t taste like vinegar at all.\n\nTraditional balsamic is very expensive (the cheapest 12-year-old costs €45), but it lasts forever and you only need a few drops to transform a dish (try it on strawberries or parmesan).\n\nWe couldn’t resist buying a bottle—not only because it tasted so good and was a rare chance to buy real balsamic in Modena, but because we wanted to support this family who is working so hard to keep the traditions alive.\n\nTours are free but you must book in advance by email or phone. See the [Acetaia di Giorgio website](http://www.acetaiadigiorgio.it/en/who-we-are/tour-to-acetaia-di-giorgio/) for details.\n\n**Eating in Modena**\n\nModena has some of the best restaurants in the world including three-Michelin-star [Osteria Francescana](https://maps.app.goo.gl/zYVHxiqibSrAjyPeA), so make sure you include a meal in your visit (and it’s a good idea to book).\n\nWe had lunch at the simple, traditional [Da Danilo](https://maps.app.goo.gl/UXoYcG7icUVG3gry8)—the pumpkin tortelloni were good if a little sweet and we loved the ricotta tortelloni in a balsamic sauce.\n\nWe also enjoyed the local sparkling red Lambrusco wine and the thick black digestif nocino (well, it grew on me). They make their own fresh pasta and only serve that and desserts, so it’s ideal if you don’t want an epic multi-course meal.\n\nThey open all day until 9pm so you can eat outside the usual strict Italian mealtimes. They also don’t have a cover charge and water is free (very rare in Italy).\n\nThe restaurant is housed in a cool and modern space with unusual lampshades and tables decorated with pots of fresh herbs.\n\nDespite this it’s a casual place—the menu is on a blackboard and you write your table number and order on the notepad and give it to the waiter then pay afterwards at the till.\n\nMost importantly, of course, the food is delicious. The menu includes traditional Bolognese dishes (tortelloni is the only vegetarian one) and a changing weekly menu of more creative plates of pasta.\n\nThere were four vegetarian options and one vegan (marked on the menu) when we visited.\n\nWe had the vegan potato *capellati* with a cherry tomato and sage sauce and black ravioli stuffed with peas and buffalo mozzarella mousse on the side.\n\nBoth were wonderful and it was refreshing to have a break from tortelloni which was the only thing we could eat in many places. All dishes are served with a side of vegetables and a roll.\n\nOur *torta tenerina*chocolate cake was excellent and even better than the one we tried in its home town Ferrara.\n\nThe only downside of Sfoglia Rina is that it’s popular, so get there early for lunch or go outside the usual 12.30–2.30pm lunch hours.\n\nWe got in without waiting at 12 pm on a Monday but an hour later it was packed, and at the same time on a Sunday, there was a 40-minute wait.\n\n*Details:* Via Castiglione 5/B. Open daily 9am – 9pm.  \n  \n*Google Maps:* [Sfoglia Rina](https://maps.app.goo.gl/g9FXQXFpCZEZBafTA)\n\n### Oltre.\n\n**Oltre** is a mix of fancy and hipster and serves traditional Bolognese food with a modern twist.\n\nThe menu is quite small and there were no vegetarian mains, but when we asked, the waiter offered us the classic tortelloni filled with ricotta in a butter and sage sauce “fuori menu” (off the menu).\n\nWe shared a creamy potato and leek soup to start then both had the tortelloni and made yummy noises with every bite—it was the best pasta in Bologna that we had on our trip.\n\nThe chocolate fondant dessert was also wonderful.\n\nIt was our most expensive meal in Bologna (€63 for two when we visited), but it’s worth it for a more upmarket experience and would be even better for meat-eaters.\n\n*Details:* Via Majani 1/A. Open Thurs – Fri 7.30 pm – 11.30 pm, Sat – Mon 12.30 pm – 2.30 pm/ 7.30 pm – 11.30 pm. Closed Tuesdays and Wednesdays.  \n  \n*Google Maps:* [Oltre](https://maps.app.goo.gl/6LYus4TA2ouFFgv78)\n\n### Trattoria del Rosso\n\n![Green tortelloni with a walnut and saffron sauce at Trattoria Del Rosso in Bologna, Italy](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns%3D%22http%3A%2F%2Fwww.w3.org%2F2000%2Fsvg%22%20viewBox%3D%220%200%201100%20825%22%3E%3C%2Fsvg%3E)\n\nGreen tortelloni with a walnut and saffron sauce\n\n**Trattoria del Rosso** is a simple, traditional restaurant that was recommended by our Airbnb host.\n\nThey had four vegetarian mains on the menu as well as two meat-free specials.\n\nWe had the gnocchi with vegetables—the gnocchi was good but the sauce a little dull—and the special green tortelloni with a walnut and saffron sauce, which was tasty but we couldn’t help comparing it to our perfect (but much more expensive) tortelloni at Oltre.\n\nOverall it’s nothing special but good for a casual, affordable meal (€41 for two at the time of our visit).\n\n*Details:* Via Augusto Righi, 30. One may say that you eat better in Modena or Parma (other cities with exceptional foods in the Emilia-Romagna). And it’s not always a given you eat well in Bologna- I’ve had my fair share of mediocre, lackluster affairs!\n\n**My lasting impressions are these:**\n\nThe richness of traditional eateries of Bologna is more palpable than in Florence, period. Partly because the cuisine is made with inherently richer, prized local ingredients.  Consider ragu’ is cooked in butter for crying out loud!\n\nYou find butter used in Florence, but it’s more of a novelty rather than something deeply rooted in tradition- after all you find more olive trees in Tuscany than in the Emilia-Romagna!\n\nNot counting the student garbage food dumps in Bologna, the restaurateurs in Bologna are catering to the Bolognese palate- which tends to lean more towards traditional home cooking they grew up on.Florence restauranteurs have a more dynamic target audience that isn’t as much based on being compared to their grandmother’s cooking.\n\nIn Florence’s center, I’d say a minimum 50% of the clientele is foreign, without this nostalgic (opinionated) reference for how the food should be tasting unlike those who study/work in the food industry in some way or who were raised with the cuisine.\n\nThis is a gross generalization, and could honestly be applied to pretty much anywhere in Italy (i.e. local diners holding traditional restauranteurs to the standards of their family culinary memories)\n\nSo I personally think a restauranteur in Florence who is doing traditional fare might simplify knowing a large % of their clientele may either not appreciate wholly genuine/true to the recipe dishes or not know the difference. I’m convinced the majority of restaurants in Florence’s city center pander to international tastes, rather than those of even their own.\n\nTuscan cuisine you find today is mostly *la cucina povera* (peasant soups, dishes)and some notes of noble and Etruscan cuisine, pappardelle an example of the Etruscans’ rich tastes.\n\nThe cuisine of the Emilia-Romagna seems to be fit for kings with all the prized ingredients which dominate their pantry- the region is home to 42 DOP products! Surely, there is interesting food in Tuscany, and I do find it more varied (especially in terms of vegetables).\n\nQuite honestly, I’d get diabetes living solely on *la cucina Bolognese*. But I think lasagna Bolognese, tortellini al brodo, mortadella and tagliatelle al ragu’ are unparalleled items for decadent indulgence compared to *la cucina contadina Toscana.*\n\nEven if Bologna has better restaurant quality food than Florence, it mostly applies to traditional cuisine. They seem even more dinosaur in strength to refuse modernizing their food scene.\n\nYou’ll find ONE Michelin-star restaurant in the whole city, Ristorante dei Portici, and it’s at a hotel. You may find a handful of restaurants serving refined versions of Bolognese cuisine, but Florence to me has some diversity in terms of experimentation and multicultural food offerings even if behind most other major cities like Milan and Rome.\n\nAt the end of the day, it may seem unfair to compare metropolis cities to Florence, but while small, has similar international appeal and draw. Plus, its the Italian city I have most experience eating in, so naturally I compare!\n\nThe only thing I found “modern” in Bologna were irritating bakeries and gimmicky “concept” hipster joints trying to imitate homogenous food trends.\n\nFrom a day-to-day perspective, I found the Bologna very *Bolognese* in terms of culinary open mindedness, habits and cultural sensitivity.\n\nDespite the fact that the world comes to Bologna to display art and perform music, it seemed like just any other Italian working city. Florence, aside from its massive American and foreign expat communities, has a notable international/artist scene. Paling in comparison to Rome’s or Milan’s, I’m sure!\n\nHere in Florence there are international institutes, academies and festivals, bringing people from all over the world. Florence is one of the few cities in Italy that is both a picturesque village and somewhat dynamic.\n\nI’d consider Florence way more accustomed to having diverse foreign communities residing there and seems to be culturally sensitive, more than I experienced in Bologna. In addition to the original Via San Felice location (with an attached fresh pasta shop) she has opened an upscale second outpost inside the historic Torre Galluzzi at Corte Galluzzi).\n\n**Ristorante al Cambio** is a surefire time capsule for local favorites from lasagna to *passatelli*, now under chef Matteo Poggi, who has refreshed the menu with modern technique while respecting tradition; their lasagna was named by the *New York Times* as one of the best pasta dishes in the world to try.\n\nFor a solid traditional pick at chef quality still an easy reach from the center, head to **Trattoria Bertozzi** run by Chef Fabio Berti (recently updated interiors, including a covered courtyard; same rich, indulgent style. Try the stuffed *balanzoni* made with red-and-blue *sfoglia* pasta, a nod to Bologna FC.\n\nFor more great traditional food with a vibe, don’t miss **Da Serghei** (loved their lasagna).  \nI think **Osteria Bottega** is reliably solid (even if on every list, I went and saw why. Super high quality and great service, even if slightly pricey!).  \n  \n**Grassilli** for one of the best cotolettas in town. My favorite place to recommend to people given the quality of dishes and its very traditional atmosphere and old-timey details.\n\n**Collegio di Spagna** for simple fare and especially fried bread pillows called *crescentine fritte* with cured meats and fresh cheese (*squacquerone*).\n\n*Curious to taste some of Bologna’s best traditional foods? In other words, if you’re into trend food scene/places—**Ahimè** is your ticket (note: **Unodiquestigiorni** is their newer, more ambitious sibling with refined service and a strong focus on intentionally sourced specialty ingredients and no-waste ethos).\n\n**Oltre** is also another modern contemporary fixture in the Bolognese dining scene.\n\n**Scaccomatto** for a little more gourmet “fusion” takes on traditional dishes and flavors, now in a permanent space inside a former monastery, with a magical garden in summer and a contemporary open kitchen in winter.\n\nFor fine dining head to **Ristorante I Portici**, still holding one Michelin star, now under Chef Nicola Annunziata, with expanded offerings including an osteria, a Neapolitan pizzeria, and a pastry shop next door.\n\nFor THE best traditional Bolognese fare, albeit a few stone throws from the city center: check **Mastrosasso**, **Bottega Aleotti**, and **Mirasole** (note: Officina del Gusto has closed).\n\nTo take a break from all the meat and partake in a great seafood meal dine at **Ristorante Acqua Pazza** by Chef Carboni.\n\n**Ristorante I Carracci** for more hotel, luxury-style fine dining, now led by Chef Agostino Schettino, and lastly **Aretusi** **Trattoria** by Chef Panichi.\n\nOther contemporary international ideas: **Ciao Kebab and** **Sentaku Ramen**\n\n**For wine lovers:**\n\nYou can’t go to Bologna without drinking at **Enoteca Faccioli**– this is my ultimate favorite wine bar which specializes in high quality minimal intervention (aka natural) wines.
