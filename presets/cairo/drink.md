Order a mixed grill and you get a sizzling tray of kofta, kebab, chicken and often lamb chops — the way to taste everything at once. Abou El Sid for refined classics, **Zooba** for street food reimagined (clearly labelled vegan, veggie and allergen options — a genuinely useful menu), and a string of rooftop bar-grills with the best river views in town.\n\n### Sayeda Zeinab and Old Cairo\n\nThe no-frills, get-it-right end. Round it out with **baharat** (a warm all-purpose blend), cinnamon, cardamom and dried mint, and you’ve got the toolkit behind most Cairo cooking.\n\nIf you want to take the flavours home, the spice stalls of Islamic Cairo around Khan el-Khalili are the place — heaped, fragrant, and far cheaper than a supermarket. A coffee or tea at an ahwa is cheap, often 20-50 EGP; a shisha adds maybe 50-100. Then there are the juice stands, which are a genuine Cairo institution: a glass of fresh-pressed mango, guava, sugarcane, orange, strawberry or pomegranate for 20-50 EGP off practically any corner. In summer a cold sugarcane (*asab*) or a thick mango shake fixes the heat instantly. In winter, swap to **sahlab**, a warm milky orchid-root drink dusted with nuts and cinnamon.\n\nA couple more worth knowing: **sobia**, a sweet coconut-milk drink that surges during Ramadan, and **‘erk sous**, a dark, intensely bitter-sweet liquorice-root drink poured by sash-wearing vendors who clack brass cups to announce themselves — try a small one for the experience even if you don’t finish it. Bottled soft drinks and very sweet packaged juices are sold everywhere, but the fresh-pressed corner stands are the move; point at the fruit you want and watch it go straight into the press.\n\nOn alcohol: Egypt is majority Muslim and many local restaurants are dry, but hotels, Western-style restaurants and some bars serve it. Koshari, ful, taameya, most mezze, stuffed vine leaves, fried aubergine, tahini and baba ghanoush are vegan as standard. Two small asks cover the edge cases: when you order ful, say “no egg, no butter” if you’re strict; and check the taameya is fried in vegetable oil. **Zooba** in Zamalek is the safe, clearly-labelled bet, and Maadi and Heliopolis have a growing crop of dedicated veg and vegan spots. A second meal, **suhoor**, runs into the small hours. Tables fill with kunafa, qatayef (stuffed pancakes) and special soups you won’t see the rest of the year. It’s one of the great food experiences on earth if you time it — book iftar tables ahead, they sell out. A few sensible habits keep it that way:\n\n* **Busy is safe.** A packed stall means high turnover and fresh ingredients. An empty one means food that’s been sitting. This single rule does most of the work.\n* **Eat it hot.** Freshly cooked, steaming food is your friend. Be cautious with raw street-stall salads and unpeeled fruit that may have been rinsed in tap water.\n* **Drink bottled water only,** and check the seal is unbroken before you open it. Skip ice unless you’re somewhere reputable that filters its water. Don’t drink the tap water.\n* **Ease in.** Don’t make the spiciest, oiliest cart your first meal off the plane. Give your stomach a day or two to adjust, and pack a basic anti-diarrhoeal just in case.\n\n## Costs, Tipping and Paying\n\nCairo is one of the best-value food cities anywhere right now — with the pound around 50 to the dollar in mid-2026, a street meal is a dollar or two and a good restaurant dinner is often under $15. Rough daily food budgets per person: **street-food only, 200-350 EGP; mixing carts and casual restaurants, 500-900 EGP; restaurants with the odd splurge, 1,200 EGP and up.** For the full money picture across your whole trip, see our [daily costs and cash-handling guide](https://www.cairotourism.org/cairo-travel-budget-cost-money-guide/).\n\nOn tipping: sit-down restaurants usually add a **service charge of around 12%** to the bill — and that mostly goes to the house, not your waiter, so it’s normal to leave another **10-15% in cash** on top for good service. At street stalls, tipping isn’t expected; rounding up is a nice touch. Tip in Egyptian pounds, in small notes. Carry cash: plenty of restaurants take cards, but carts, market stalls and small joints are cash-only. A few words go a long way too — *shukran* (thank you) and *akl gameel* (lovely food) earn real smiles and sometimes a bigger scoop. For centuries, the thousands of coffeehouses and cafes in Cairo have served as important social hubs, and today, the city is thronging with cafes of all shapes and sizes. Most common is the *ahwa baladi,* the traditional coffeehouse found on street corners in every neighbourhood. Each day, millions of Egyptians gather at the ahwa to chat, discuss the issues of the day, watch [football](https://www.cntravellerme.com/story/the-hidden-football-stadium-disguised-amongst-venices-canals), or simply pass time. These neighbourhood cafes are usually no-nonsense affairs, with hot coffee or cold Pepsi served alongside *argileh* (shisa), often on plastic tables that sprawl out into the street.\n\nAlongside sampling a traditional ahwa, visitors to Cairo might also want to seek out the city’s more famous [historical](https://www.cntravellerme.com/story/best-things-to-do-in-cairo) and cultural cafes. In Downtown Cairo, several iconic cafes played an important role as spaces where intellectuals, activists and artists met over the years to share ideas or plot revolutions. Like much of central Cairo, many of these historic spaces leave an impression of faded grandeur, but visiting them is a good way to conjure up the country’s rich cultural history. More recently, modern cafes have sprung up across Cairo, including both Western chains and their local competitors such as Beano’s and Cilantro. Other independent cafes can be found in hotel gardens or connected to galleries and bookshops, serving as cultural hubs and co-working spaces where Egyptians and visitors alike can spend their days in respite from the city’s busy streets.\n\n## The best cafes in Cairo\n\n1. Since opening in 1908, the cafe has operated as an intellectual hub where writers, artists and revolutionaries gather to exchange ideas over Egyptian Stella or coffee. The legendary Egyptian author Naguib Mahfouz, whose Nobel Prize-winning works paint a vivid social history of Cairo, was a regular at the cafe and hosted weekly salons there. His portrait occupies centre stage in the side room of the cafe, where the walls are covered with pictures of famous Egyptian intellectuals and artists over the ages. Today, Cafe Riche feels like a relic of a bygone era. It is usually quiet, with couples chatting in hushed tones over a drink, while several servers dressed in historic Nubian attire wait around aimlessly. The food is mediocre - visit instead for a quick drink while paying homage to Egypt’s intellectual giants.\n\n  **Address:** 17 Talaat Harb street, Downtown, Cairo\n* Photo Tommy Hilton\n\n  ## Eish & Malh\n\n  Eish & Malh, meaning bread and salt, offers excellent food and great coffee in a restored building in downtown Cairo. Broad, open windows look out across the road at the Sha’ar HaShamayim Synagogue, built in 1908 in a style evoking Pharaonic temples. Despite the armed police guarding the synagogue and the rush of cars along the street, the cafe has a relaxed, informal atmosphere, with high ceilings and simple red decor. There is plenty of space to work or catch up with friends, including in the more secluded upstairs section. The [brunch](https://www.cntravellerme.com/story/best-brunch-restaurants-in-beirut) menu offers delicious eggs and shakshuka alongside sweet treats including pancakes, waffles, and three varieties of french toast.
