It was in the Bassin that Pascal has his first pastry shop and these bites of heaven were invented. As the story goes, Pascal’s son came home having had a little too much wine early one morning. Pascal was already making the pastry known as chouquettes for the day, and his son grabbed the chantilly cream and filled a chouquette with it. Whether the story is true, we’ll never know for sure. But if it is, we have Pascal’s son’s druken escapades to thank.\n\nYou can get the Dunes Blanches by the piece. There’s no tables to sit and enjoy them, but you can take them around the corner to the Place Gambetta and enjoy them in the park like many Bordelaise do. Or [L’Alchimiste](http://alchimiste-cafes.com/) is a wonderful coffee shop kitty-corner from the patstry shop and just happen to sell Pascal’s dunes blanches as well.       "raw_content": "# [The Wine Chaser](https://thewinechaser.wordpress.com)\n\n## Restaurant terminology and slang\n\n## A Glossary for Front of House\n\n**A Glossary of more than 500 terms** used in restaurants and hospitality.\n\nI’ve worked in a number of restaurants, bars and pubs both in the UK and Canada and enjoy that hospitality has its own lingo. Some terms are used universally, some are used in specific styles of restaurant and there are differences in different countries. I have grouped together the most comprehensive list of terminology and job titles that are predominantly used by Front of house staff in restaurants but also includes a lot of kitchen terminology that you need to know. This includes some cooking terms that are commonly found on menus.\n\nI hope it’s useful for people getting into the business and maybe baffled by some of the terms heard around the restaurant. Also check out my [Bar Terminology Glossary](https://thewinechaser.wordpress.com/2024/03/27/bar-terminology/) and [Sommelier’s Wine Terminology](https://thewinechaser.wordpress.com/2024/09/29/sommeliers-wine-glossary/) pages.\n\n[A](#A) [B](#BB) [C](#CC) [D](#DD) [E](#EE) [F](#FF) [G](#GG) [H](#HH) [I](#II) [J](#JJ) [K](#KK) [L](#LL) [M](#MM) [N](#NN) [O](#OO) [P](#PP) [Q](#QQ) [R](#RR) [S](#SS) [T](#TT) [U](#UU) [V](#VV) [WXYZ](#WW)\n\n**#-Out –** *See **Out***.\n\n**#-Pan** **–** A particular size of pan used in a professional kitchen when proceeded by a number. *e.g. a 5**-pan** or a 9**-pan.***\n\n**68 –** When something has been ***86***‘ed but is now back to being available again within a given service. The opposite of***86*****.**\n\n**85** – Almost  out of something. *q.v.* ***86****.*\n\n**86** – Something has run out and is not available to order. *e.g. ‘Tarte Tatin is* ***86****‘ed’* or something that has to be taken off the menu.\n\n## A\n\n**Aboyeur –** *See* ***Expo/******Expediter***. French – literarily “barker or shouter” Shouts out the orders to the ***brigade*** as they come in to the kitchen. A role often taken by the ***sous chef*** or ***Head chef.***\n\n**ABS –** Short for ***Always be scanning.***\n\n**AGM – Assistant General Manager*****.*** Also known as a ***DGM***/***Deputy General Manager*** in some restaurants, but these can potentially be two different roles.\n\n**À la carte** – Menu where individual courses can be ordered freely. *q.v.* ***Se******t menu***\n\n**À la ding –** Slang for cooked in a microwave. *q.v. **Chef Mike.***\n\n**À la minute** – A dish or part of a dish that is cooked from scratch when ordered, with no prep beforehand. *q.v. **Made to order.***\n\n**À la mode –** US and Canada only – for a dessert served with ice cream. *e.g. ‘Blueberry pie **à la mode’*** means a blueberry pie is served with ice cream.\n\n**Al dente –** (Pronounced *al dentay*) Italian term. widely used in anglophone countries. This indicates that there is some firmness to pasta or vegetables after cooking.\n\n**Alfresco dining –** Eating outside, usually on a restaurant ***patio*** or terrace.\n\n**Allergens –** Most countries have a legal requirement to list key allergens on a menu or if asked if used. The US has 9 ‘key allergens’, Canada has 11 ‘priority allergens’ and the UK and EU have 14 ‘major allergens’ that must be listed or the information must be available to customers. This is part of ***compliance*** within the restaurant. *q.v.* ***dietaries***; ***Segregated prep.***\n\n**All day** – A term which broadly means ‘In total’ with two slightly different uses.  \n**1.** Used in the kitchen referring to how many of something is needed to be made at any one time *i.e.* currently on order. e.g. If three separate orders come in, each with 2 x Chicken parmigiana on them, the ***expo*** could shout *‘I need 6 Chicken parmigiana all day.’*  \n**2.** It can also refer to how many of an item is left in stock to order for that service. *e.g. ‘I’ve got 8 T-bone steaks **all day**‘* means there are 8 T-bone steaks left before they have run out and need to be ***86′***ed.\n\n**Alongside** – Usually accompanied by ‘*on your right/left*‘. A warning called out to someone when approaching their side, usually holding plates/tray of drinks to make sure they don’t step into you.\n\n**Always be scanning –** A familiar motto used for ***FOH*** workers, that encourages constant vigilance, watching of your ***section*** or throughout the ***floor***, looking at tables, checking for anything that needs doing or a guest that is trying to flag someone down. *AKA **ABS**; q.v. **Eyes down**.*\n\n**American service –** The most common form of service in modern restaurants. The food is plated in the kitchen and taken to the guest’s table and served by a ***server.*** *AKA* ***Plated service; Table service, Sit-down service.***\n\n**Amuse (Bouche)** – Pronounced *‘amooz (boosh)’*. Literally ‘amuse the mouth’. A small bite specially made for a customer that is often ***off-menu***. Usually offered by the restaurant as a favour to a ***VIP*** or for a special occasion.\n\n**Aperitif –** A drink that is served before any food is ordered or served. Usually a cocktail, sparkling wine or gin & tonic but potentially any drink taken before a meal. *q.v.* ***Digestif***.\n\n**Appetizer**/**Appetiser** – A small amount of food with two slightly different meanings  \n **1.** Small bites of food served before a meal such as olives, nuts and other snacks that are placed in the middle of the table in small bowls for guests to share.  \n **2.** The first course or starter of a standard three-course meal. (Mainly US meaning).\n\n**Appy –** *(Slang)* – Short for ***appetizer*** (Mainly North America).\n\n**Assiette of desserts** – (sometimes *Assiette à desserts*) – literally a *plate of desserts*, is a platter of several different miniature ***desserts***, ***cakes*** or ***puddings*** served for the ***sweet course*** of a meal. It is curated and showcases the chef’s or ***pastry chef***‘s skill and usually includes four desserts of differing styles, flavours and textures. *AKA **Assiette of puddings***.\n\n**Wrap to-go –** Wrapping up any leftover food in a ***doggy bag*** or ***to-go box*** for a ***guest*** to take home and eat later. *e.g. ‘May I have the rest of my food **wrapped to go**?’*\n\n**Assiette of puddings**  – *See **Assiette of desserts.***\n\n**Assistant Head Sommelier** – The person who serves as a deputy to the ***Head Sommelier***, assisting in all duties pertaining to the wine list, cellar, ordering and wine service, as well as other beverage/bar management when required. *q.v.* ***sommelier.***\n\n**Assistant sommelier** – See ***Commis sommelier***.\n\n**Assistant waiter/Assistant server –** An alternative name for a***busser***, ***commis waiter***, ***food runner*** or ***back waiter.*** junior members of the ***wait staff*** who don’t take orders from customers but ***bus*** or ***run*** food from the ***pass*** to the table, as well as help clear tables. They also hold the trays of dishes when a restaurant uses ***tray service.*** They may well have other ***side work*** duties like polishing ***cutlery*** or folding napkins/***linens***.\n\n**Auction** – When you come to at a table with food or drinks and don’t know who has what and have to ask, customers usually raise a hand like in an auction. *e.g. ‘Who’s having the beef ? Who’s having the chicken?’* *AKA* ***Call out (dishes).***\n\n**Au gratin –** (Pronounced *Oh-Grattan*) Refers to a dish (usually vegetables or pasta) topped with grated cheese, butter and breadcrumbs and is baked or grilled/broiled so it has a crispy, golden crust.\n\n**Autograt –** An automatic ***gratuity***. Many restaurants add a mandatory ***service charge*** to the ***bill/check*** for large ***parties***, or for private events.\n\n**Au jus –** (Pronounced *Oh-zhoo*) The meal is served with a sauce or type of light, thin gravy using the meat juices.\n\n**Average check –** A calculation of the average amount spent by table, as opposed to a ***per person average/per guest average.***\n\n**Away** – Used as a term to make and serve drinks/food ASAP when they have been ***held***. Courses are usually held waiting for the tables to be cleared of the previous course. Drinks can be held for a particular course to be served, especially when part of a ***pairing***. *e.g. ‘Drinks **away** on Table 60′* *q.v. **Fire.***\n\n## B\n\n**Backed up** – When you have a lot of orders waiting to be prepared or made. Usually indicated by a crowded ***rail*** jam-packed full of order ***tickets***.\n\n**Back of house / BOH** – Broadly speaking refers to the kitchen and its staff. *q.v. **Brigade.***\n\n**Backs** – Called out in warning when you are behind someone, especially if you or they are carrying a tray or plates. If you hear this don’t step backwards or you can move forward slightly out of the way! *q.v.* ***behind***.\n\n**Back waiter –** An alternative name for a ***busser***, ***commis waiter***,  ***food runner*** or ***Assistant waiter.*** junior members of the ***wait staff*** who don’t take orders from customers but ***bus*** or ***run*** food from the ***pass*** to the table, as well as help clear tables. They also hold the trays of dishes when a restaurant uses ***tray service.*** They may well have other ***side work*** duties like polishing ***cutlery*** or folding napkins/***linens***.\n\n**Bain-marie –** (Pronounced *banh marree*)A type of water bath or double boiler, that uses heated water to gently cook or keep food items warm. It consists of a larger vessel that contains the heated water and an inner vessel that contains the food and a lid. There are electric versions as well as those that sit on a gas burner.\n\n**Ballantine –** A deboned cut of poultry, usually chicken, duck or turkey that is rolled, stuffed and tied up in a cylinder shape and cooked. It can be stuffed with anything including herbs, bread stuffing, mushrooms or vegetables.\n\n**Bank –** **1.** *See **Server’s bank***.  \n**2.** Shortened form of ***Banquette.***\n\n**Banquet –** A formal dinner for a large number of guests, usually in a special venue but can be hosted at a restaurant.\n\n**Banquet chef –** A ***chef*** whospecialises in large**b*****anquets***, typically at an events venue.\n\n**Banquet Event Order** – A specific ***event sheet*** or ***function sheet*** containing all the relevant information and requirements for a ***Banquet*** or other event. *AKA* ***BEO*.**\n\n**Banquet seating –** A style of seating plan for events where large round tables (‘***Rounds***‘) are used and chairs are placed evenly around the table *q.v.* ***Family-style seating, Cabaret-style seating***.\n\n**Banquette** – (pronounced ‘*bæng-kett*‘) An upholstered bench seat against a wall with a table in front, and chairs on the opposite side. Sometimes shortened to *‘**bank**‘*\n\n**Barback** – Someone who works at the bar but usually not making the drinks, although they can when asked. They are usually washing/polishing glassware, prepping garnishes, keeping fridges stocked, replenishing ice, washing cocktail tools. Often ***bussing*** tables to get glassware back to the bar or glass-washer.\n\n**Bar manager –** Person in charge of the bar and drinks service. May not include being in charge of wine as that is the job of a ***sommelier*** or the ***Head Sommelier*** in some restaurants, although may well oversee wines served by the glass.\n\n**Bar mop –** A type of heavy-duty cloth made from terry towelling to wipe up spills and for general cleaning around the bar, restaurant and kitchen. *q.v. **Side towel**.*\n\n**Bar napkin –** See ***Cocktail napkin*** or ***Bevnap***\n\n**Bar Service** – Usually refers to a bar or pub where drinks and sometimes food have to be ordered at the bar with a bartender rather than via a ***server*** at the table. *AKA* ***Counter service*.** *q.v. **Table service***\n\n**Bar team –** The collective term for the ***bartenders***, ***barbacks***, and supervisors or bar managers that work behind the bar at a restaurant. *q.v **Floor team.***\n\n**Bartender –** Person who works behind the bar or ***service bar***. They will pour and serve drinks as well as make cocktails. They will work under a ***Bar manager*** or sometimes the ***Head sommelier*** in a restaurant. They may work with a ***barback*** in larger or busier bars and restaurants.\n\n**Beer Sommelier –** A specialist in beer and beer service, usually working in (craft) beer-focussed bars and restaurants. They will have passed exams and have certifications to demonstrate that expertise. *q.v.* ***Cicerone; Sommelier***\n\n**Behind** – Called out in warning when you are behind someone, especially if you or they are carrying a tray or plates. Sometimes accompanied by *‘with hot!’ or ‘**hot behind**!’* if a cook is carrying something hot in the kitchen. *q.v.* ***B******acks***; ***Hot behind.***\n\n**BEO –** Short for ***Banquet Event Order***. A specific ***event sheet*** or ***function sheet*** containing all the relevant information and requirements for a ***Banquet*** or other event.\n\n**Beverage napkin –** *See **Bevnap.***\n\n**Bevnap** – Short for ***Beverage napkin***. A small black or white paper napkin used to serve drinks on or for general use at the bar. Also known as a ‘***cocktail napkin***‘.\n\n**Bib apron –** A full length apron that has a ‘bib part’ to it that covers the body above the waist as well as below the waist. *q.v. **Bistro apron; Server’s apron.***\n\n**Big-top –** A big round table for lots of people or large ***parties***. *q.v.* ***Top***\n\n**Bill** – Usually in the UK rather than North America. An itemised run down on paper of what has been ordered, the cost and service charges. This is the total that is owed for the meal and drinks by a given table. *q.v. **Check/Cheque***.\n\n**Bill-fold** – (or ***Bill-folder***) A leather, plastic or cardboard holder for the ***bill*** or ***check*** to go in when presented to a customer or ***dropped*** at a table. *AKA* ***Check presenter*.**\n\n**Bisque –** A type of thick, creamy soup usually made with fish or shellfish stock.\n\n**Bistro** *(also spelled* **Bistrot) –** A French term for a casual, neighbourhood restaurant serving good value, simple food, wine and other alcoholic beverages . Some ***bistros*** may allow customers to just sit and drink as well. When the term is used outside of France, it usually indicates that it serves French-style dishes. *q.v. **Osteria**; **Brasserie.***\n\n**Bistro apron –** A half-length apron that covers the legs from the waist down and does not cover the body above the waist like a ***bib apron.*** It usually covers most of the legs and is longer than a ***server’s apron.*** It should have pockets for ***order pads*** and pens etc. as it is usually worn by ***servers***.\n\n**Black and Blue –** Extra rare cooking for a steak. ie. seared briefly on the outside and cool in the middle.\n\n**Block –** Blocking tables means not allowing them to be booked or reserved on the ***booking system***, so they can be kept free for in-house reservations, ***walk-in******s*** etc. or to limit capacity.\n\n**Blocking Sheet** – A list of all the reservations and times/tables allocated and for how long. Also known as a ***Reservation Sheet***.\n\n**Blue plate special –** A term only used in the US and Canada. An inexpensive, discounted meal that changes daily, commonly available in ***diners.***\n\n**Blue roll –** A large roll of thin blue tissue paper used around restaurant kitchens and behind bars for general drying hands, cleaning and wiping up. *q.v. **C-folds***.\n\n**Board –** A name sometimes used for the ***rail*** or ***tab grabber*** that holds all the order ***tickets,*** especially in the kitchen.\n\n**BOH –** *See* ***Back of House.***\n\n**Booking** – A reservation made by a guest for a table, ***PDR*** or private event. *q.v.* ***reso***; ***event sheet***.\n\n**Booking system** – The computer program or application used to take ***bookings***/reservations, keep ***guest notes*** and allocate tables.\n\n**Booth** – A partially enclosed seating area/table for diners, usually on three sides or else in a corner known as a ‘***corner booth***‘\n\n**Bottle bin –** A large plastic bin, sometimes on wheels where empty glass bottles are put during service, usually behind a bar, ready for recycling or disposal.\n\n**Bottle dump cooler/fridge –** (sometimes ***dump cooler/fridge***) A large chest fridge that is used in a bar where large numbers of bottles of wine, sparkling wine or beer etc. can be chilled standing up. Useful for very busy bars or pubs and for venues that host a lot of large events.\n\n**Bottomless brunch –** A period of ***service*** between breakfast and lunch when a promotional price is charged that covers as much as you can eat or drink (usually *Prosecco)*. This is an extremely busy and challenging ***shift***. The stuff of ***servers***‘ nightmares!\n\n**Box a table cloth** – A method of setting a table cloth on a square or rectangular table so that the edges are folded flat against the legs of the table, rather than hanging loosely over the corners, sometimes held in place with clips.\n\n**Braised / Braising –** A cooking method that uses a combination of methods. A cut of meat (or sometimes vegetables) is first seared on high heat, sometimes followed by covered cooking, then partially covered in a small amount of liquid to cook slowly in a covered pot until the meat is tender. Usually used for tougher cuts of meat. *q.v. **Stewing.***\n\n**Brasserie –** Literally French for a brewery. When used in a restaurant sense it means a semi-casual restaurant, but usually slightly more formal and larger than a ***bistro.*** Originally it would have brewed its own beer on site as well, but this is not true in the modern sense.\n\n**Bread plate –** A small ***side plate*** used for bread that is served with or before a meal.\n\n**Bread service –** The term to describe the serving of bread before or with a meal. It is usually served with butter or olive oil and balsamic vinegar. Bread service is usually complimentary or can be paid for by a ***cover charge.***\n\n**Briefing** – A quick meeting before a ***service*** to let staff know relevant information. Usual information would be ***86s***, number of ***covers*, *dietries***, ***VIPs*,** customer ***flow*** as well as ***specials***, new menu items or any drinks/food to ***push***. *AKA **Pre-shift briefing; Pre-service briefing.*** *q.v. **Briefing template.***\n\n**Briefing template –** A pre-printed sheet with preset topics and points that need to be covered in a ***pre-shift briefing***. *q.v. **Briefing.***\n\n**Brigade (de Cuisine) –** The kitchen team. Includes all roles from ***Chef de Cuisine/head chef*** to all junior roles such as the ***KP.*** *q.v.* ***Brigade System*.**\n\n**Brigade system –** A system of organising the kitchen staff, based on a strict hierarchy and division of labour/jobs. Based on French Chef Escoffier, an ex-military chef who reorganised and modernised chaotic French kitchens in the late 19th Century and ran famous hotel kitchens along the lines of this system in London, most notably the Savoy, Ritz and Carlton. *q.v. **Brigade (de Cuisine)***.\n\n**Beurre Blanc –** A sauce made by combining white wine, vinegar, shallots heating, reducing and whisking in butter to emulsify it. One of the classic sauces of classic French cuisine.\n\n**Buffet Pan –** *See* ***Hotel pan.***\n\n**Buffet service –** Food service where the food is prepared and served on sideboards or on hotplates, ***chafing dishes***, or under food warmer lamps and guests help themselves before returning to their table. *q.v. **Stand-up Buffet; Cafeteria.***\n\n**Bump (it) –** Moving a ***ticket*** or ***chit*** off the ***KDS*** or ***rail*** once it has been made and served or else moving it to another status on the ***bump bar.***\n\n**Bump bar –** A panel of buttons attached to a ***Kitchen Display System*** (***KDS***) that can ‘***bump***‘ order ***tickets*** from one status to another or prioritise them if required urgently.\n\n**Buried –** Completely overwhelmed by customers or orders. Well and truly ***In the weeds***. *q.v.* ***Going down***; ***drowning.***\n\n**Burn the ice /Burn the well** – To pour hot water over the ice in the ice-well behind the bar. This is usually done if there is a chance of contamination of the ice with broken glass or food/drink, or else at the end of service as part of a ***clear-down.***\n\n**Bus**/**Bussing**– To carry something to a table or more often, to ***clear*** and carry plates/glasses from a table back to the ***dish pit***\n\n**Bus-pan**/**Bus-tub –** A plastic rectangular tub for carrying dirty glasses/plates to the kitchen or ***dish-pit***.\n\n**Bus-tub strainer –** A slotted plastic insert that sits in a ***bus-tub*** that can be lifted out, straining out the water and lifting out the contents. Usually used for a ***cutlery*** pre-soak in the ***dish-pit.***\n\n**Busser** – A person who ***busses***. i.e. clears tables and can potentially run food/drinks, but doesn’t take orders from guests. Some definitions have bussers as purely role that clears and cleans tables. Rather archaically still called *bus-boy* by some restaurants. *q.v.* ***food runner; commis waiter***\n\n**Butler service** – A form of service with two different meanings.  \n**1.** A form of service where food is taken to the table and the diners’ side in serving dishes and the customer then serves themselves with serving spoons. Quite rare these days, but still seen with dining in ***banquet*** events or in private homes.   \n**2.** Sometimes refers to ***Cocktail service*** where ***servers*** walk around with trays of ***canapés*** and drinks at a reception. A more recent use of the term.\n\n**Butler’s tray –** A large service tray, often with integral handles either side, that goes on a foldable, portable stand or ***tray jack.*** It is usually used in ***fine dining*** restaurants and carried next to the guest’s table to serve and decant wine, to carry multiple plates so they can be served at once or to carve meat from a joint amongst a myriad of other things. *q.v.* ***Gueridon.***\n\n**Butler’s Pantry –** Today it’s a storeroom for ***linens, crockery***, ***cutlery*** and glassware/***stemware*** in a restaurant that can also include ***dry stock*** *(such as napkins, straws, ***blue roll***, ***C-folds*** & cleaning products).* Historically it’s a storeroom in a large house with servants that sits between a kitchen and dining room or a storeroom located near the kitchen where ***crockery***, glassware and ***silverware*** were stored and perhaps an area for a butler to perform administrative work (in a large stately home) separate from the food ***pantry***.\n\n**Button –** The point on a touch-screen ***POS*** that is labelled with an item name. This alerts the ***BOH*** team of incoming dishes to be prepared or ***fired***. *AKA **Check-on***. *q.v.* ***Ordering***.\n\n**Ticket spindle –** The metal ***spike*** used to stab ***tickets*** or ***chits*** on after they have been made and served. *AKA* ***Check spindle; Spike.***\n\n**Ticket time –** The time that has elapsed between an order being ***sent*** by a ***server*** and that order being completed and served to a guest. Some ***POS*** systems record this information and it is used as a metric to judge efficiency, especially in fast-paced ***casual dining*** and ***QSR*** restaurants\n\n**Tip** – A gratuity, usually a percentage of the bill added on for the staff, not the restaurant or its owners. *q.v.* ***Gratuity***; ***Tip-pool***; ***Service charge.***\n\n**Tip out –** Usually a percentage of a ***server’s*** tips gets distributed amongst other staff like ***bussers*** and ***host***, sometimes ***BOH*** staff will be included. A less formal method of tip sharing when compared to ***Tip pooling.***\n\n**Tip-pool/pooling –** A system where by ***tips*** are counted up for the whole ***service*** or ***shift*** and then allocated amongst staff according to hours worked and a points system or ratio. This ensures that ***BOH*** staff and other non-servers in the front of house get a portion of the ***gratuities.***\n\n**To-go / Togo –** Used when referring to food that has been prepped and packed to be taken away and eaten at home. *AKA **Take away.***\n\n**To-go box –** A specifically designed plastic/foil/cardboard box to ***take away*** left over food. *q.v. **Doggy bag.***\n\n**To order –***See **Made to order.***\n\n**Top** – **1. #-Top.** Used in combination with a number referring to how many seats or guests seated at a table. e.g *2-top, 4-top* etc.   \n**2.** Also refers to the actual interchangeable table-top you can put on the table legs to allow various numbers of guests. i.e. one set of legs with a ‘*4-top*‘ on it may be upgraded to a ‘*6-top*‘ by putting on a larger table-***top***. *q.v*. ***Rounds.***\n\n**Top-up** – ***Servers*** and ***sommeliers*** keep the glass of a guest filled up at their table with wine and sometimes water from a bottle, carafe or decanter.\n\n**Torchon –** French term for a robust ***side-towel*** used in the kitchen as a cloth for handling hot pots etc. and wiping down plates. Sometimes used in English-speaking restaurants.\n\n**Touching tables –** Briefly visiting tables in your section just to check-in on the guests and make sure there are no issues. *e.g ‘I’m just going to touch all the tables in my* ***section****‘*. *q.v.* ***Sweep***; ***walk by.***\n\n**Tourné –** A lozenge-shaped cut for vegetables such as potatoes, carrots and parsnips etc.\n\n**Tout suite** – Immediately! *e.g. ‘I need those canapés **tout suite**!’*, Sometimes *tout de suite*.\n\n**Trail –** When a senior manager or chef follows a new member of staff, perhaps during a ***stage*** or a ***trial shift*** to check out their skills or proficiency.\n\n**Trattoria –** An Italian term for a semi-casual restaurant, offering good food at a reasonable price. When used outside Italy, the restaurant will offer Italian style cuisine. It is generally more formal and may be larger than an ***Osteria*.**\n\n**Tray jack –** Folding stand that is used with a large serving tray or ***Butler’s tray.***\n\n**Tray liner –** A linen cloth or rubber matting that is laid on a metal trays to give them grip and stop glassware from sliding around.\n\n**Tray service** – **1.** (food) Style of service where all the dishes for a table are taken from ***the pass*** to the table on a large serving tray or ***Butler’s tray***, usually by a ***commis waiter*** or ***food runner*** and they are then placed in front of the customer by a ***server*** or ***waite******r/waitress***.   \n**2.** This term can also refer to any ***table service*** where a tray is used to deliver food or beverages to a table.\n\n**Tray trolley –** A tall, mobile rack that holds multiple trays or sometimes ***bus-pans***. Often used by corporate ***caterers*** for ***canteens***. *AKA **Clearing trolley.***\n\n**Trial Shift** – An unpaid shift in a restaurant for the ***FOH*** managers to assess a job applicant’s suitability and skills before offering them a full time position. *q.v.* ***Stage***\n\n**Triple (shift) –** A rare and very long day of working three ***services*** in a row. i.e. breakfast, lunch and dinner. *q.v.* ***Double***.\n\n**Triple seating –** When 3 tables are seated at the same time in one ***section***. This means a tough time for the ***server*** to get them served quickly. Generally servers will discourage the ***host/hostess*** from doing this! *q.v. **Double seating.***\n\n**Tron –** A shortened slang term of ***Waitron***, a now defunct un-gendered term for a ***waitress*** or ***waiter***, used in the 80s/90s.\n\n**Tronc** – A system used in the UK where all ***service charges*** are pooled and distributed via payroll. It allows the ***gratuities*** to be taxed. Many jobs advertise an annual salary, but in reality around 20% to 50% of it is paid via the ***tronc*** payments, not the restaurant itself.\n\n**Turn (Tables)** – When a table has more than one ***seating*** in a single ***service***, it needs to be cleaned and ***reset*** for the next guest. *e.g. ‘we need to* ***turn*** *tables 10, 12, 20 and 32 tonight’* *q.v.* ***relay; flip***.\n\n**Turn and Burn** – In casual dining when getting guests served and finished quickly to maximise sales and turnover of tables. (Mostly North American)\n\n**Turnover rate –** *See **Table turnover rate.***\n\n**Turnover time –** The amount of time a party or guest gets to be sat, served and vacate their table. This time ranges from well under an hour in ***quick service restaurants*** to two and half hours in ***fine dining***. *q.v. **Table turnover rate.***\n\n## U\n\n**Ullage –** **1.** Beverage wastage that can be written off against tax or other costs. This can be due to leakage or deterioration, or else used in accounting to account for wastage when pulling beer through the lines or spilled during pouring into drip-trays.   \n**2.** The air space in a wine bottle (or barrel). Older wines will show more space from evaporation or cork absorption. The lower the level the more likely the wine may be spoiled.\n\n**Up** – Ready – in the context of food or sometimes drinks. *e.g. ‘Food’s **up** for table 2′* or *‘order **up**!’*\n\n**Upscale dining –** A term used to describe fine-dining in a more relaxed atmosphere. sometimes called *Upscale-casual dining.* *AKA **Fine-casual dining** etc.*\n\n**Upcharge –** a supplemental charge or a premium for something which is not standard e.g. gluten-free pizza crust or for a premium meal on a set menu *q.v. **Supp.***\n\n**Upsell –** The act of a server enticing a customer to spend more money or upgrade to more expensive dish or wine with their table-side chat.\n\n**Usher –** A person who is either a ***host/hostess*** of a restaurant or works alongside a ***host*** or ***Maitre d’*** to greet guests and show them to their table and take their coats etc. A term only occasionally used in a restaurant. At an event an ***usher*** may have a similar role, but there might be multiple ushers to look after guests.\n\n## V\n\n**Velouté –** A light, silky sauce made from stock and a roux. Literally French for ‘velvety’.\n\n**Vertical drinking/Vertical dining –** Drinking and occasionally eating standing up sometimes at ***high top*** tables but usually without tables. Also refers to drinking while standing up at a bar.\n\n**Verbal/Verbally –** See ***Vocalise***.\n\n**Verbal tip –** A customer who leaves no ***tip*** but enthuses verbally about the great service.\n\n**Vibe –** Term used to describe what the character or feeling of a table is. *e.g. ‘What’s the **vibe** on table 22?’* – *‘Looks like they’re big spenders, may splash-out on a decent bottle of wine.’* Also used for the general atmosphere on a given service. *e.g. ‘What’s the **vibe** like in your section tonight?’*.\n\n**VIP** – Very Important Person. Usually noted on the reservation in the ***guest notes***. Ideally everyone gets the best service, but VIPs may get a complimentary drink or an ***amuse bouche***. Other similar terms are used such as *HP (High profile) or* ***PON (Person of Note)*** etc.\n\n**Vocalise** – To relay an order or repeat an order ***verbally*** to the person making it. *AKA* ***Verbal.***\n\n**Void an item –** This is to take an item off a bill on a ***POS*** as it wasn’t made or was cancelled after it was ***sent*** and there is no loss to the restaurant.\n\n**Vulture(s)** **–** *(slang)* Restaurant staff that gather around a ***misfired*** plate or even a cocktail to grab a bite or a sip. *q.v. **Seagulling.***\n\n## W\n\n**Waiter/Waitress** – Front of house ***service staff*** who serve food & drinks at the table, take orders and ***drop*** the ***bill/check***. *q.v.****Server***; ***Front waiter.***\n\n**Waiter captain –** S*ee **Captain.***\n\n**Waiter’s Cloth –** A cotton/linen cloth or towel used to serve hot plates with *AKA **Server’s cloth.*** *q.v. **Service linen***; ***Liteau.***\n\n**Waiter’s gloves –** White, or sometimes black, close-fitting cotton gloves for ***servers*** to use while carrying plates to a table during ***service***. Some claim some form of heat resistance. Usually used in some ***fine-dining*** restaurants. *AKA **Serving gloves.***\n\n**Waiter service –***See **Table service/Sit-down service.***\n\n**Waiter station –** *See **Station.***\n\n**Wait List/Waiting List** – Some very popular, usually ***fine-dining***, restaurants are fully booked months in advance and have a list of guests they can call to come in at short notice if a ***booking*** cancels.\n\n**Waitron –** A non-gendered term for a ***waiter*** or ***waitress*** that briefly saw some popularity in the 1990s. Now rarely heard as ***server*** has become the default un-gendered term widely used in the industry. Sometimes shortened to ***Tron.***\n\n**Wait staff** – Sometimes *Wait team* or ***Service staff***. The team of staff who look after front of house, serving guests at their tables and attending to their needs. Includes ***waiters/servers*, *commis waiters, captains, chefs de rang*** and sometimes the ***Floor manager*** and ***wine team/sommeliers.***\n\n**Walked – 1.** Describing a customer who has left without paying *AKA* ***Dine and dash.*** *q.v.* ***Walkout.***   \n**2.**  Describing a member of staff who quits and leaves mid-shift.\n\n**Walk by –** A quick walk over to a particular table to check on them without engaging or talking to them. *q.v.* ***Sweep; Touching tables.***\n\n**Walk-in** – **1.** Customers who don’t have a reservation and come in hopeful for a table with no prior warning.  \n**2.** Short for walk-in fridge or cooler, the large refrigerator used as the main chilled food storage unit.\n\n**Walking** – Called out (usually in the kitchen) in reply when a dish is ***complete*** and on its way to the ***pass*** or from the pass to a table, or if a part of a dish is ready and on its way to be ***plated***. *e.g.”Where’s the Fillet mignon? I need to plate now!” – ‘**walking** Chef!*‘\n\n**Walking-in –** Called out when a new order is being delivered to a kitchen by a ***server,*** usually in restaurants that don’t have an electronic ordering system or ***POS*** that automatically sends order ***tickets*** to the kitchen etc. *q.v. **Ordering.***\n\n**Walkout –** **1.** A diner or ***guest*** who walks out without paying, also known as a ***dine and dash***. *q.v. **Walked.***  \n**2**. Sometimes a guest that is ***seated*** but then leaves before ordering anything.\n\n**Walk-thru/through** – A quick walking tour by a manager or ***head chef*** to check up that everything is where it should be before service. Usually in the kitchen but sometimes refers to ***FOH***.\n\n**Water a table**– To pour the initial glasses of water for a table just after they sit down before any orders are taken.\n\n**Wax a table** – To chat to a table of guests and make them feel welcome and special.\n\n**Weeded –** Past tense of ***In the weeds.*** *e.g. ‘I was totally **weeded** by 8.30 last night and couldn’t catch up.”*\n\n**Wet Ice –** An ***ice bucket*** filled with ice and water so that bottles can be partially or completely submerged. This is used to quickly chill wines down that are too warm or keep certain wines very well-chilled. *q.v. **Dry ice***\n\n**Wetnap –**  Short for ‘wet napkin’. The packaged moist towelettes that some restaurants give out to clean your hands.\n\n**Wheel –** A circular ***board, tab grabber*** or ***rail*** where order ***tickets*** or ***chits*** are posted in a kitchen. Not seen as often any more. *q.v.* ***Wheelman***\n\n**Wheelman –** American slang for an ***Expo***. Named after the old-fashioned ***wheel*** that holds order ***tickets/chits***.\n\n**Wine cooler** – **1.** A walled metal or plastic container for bottles that keeps wine relatively cool with no ice that can be put on tables. Metal wine coolers can be kept in the freezer before being used for better effectiveness.  \n**2.** Sometimes used as a term for a ***wine fridge***, especially in North America.  \n**3.** A type of premixed, wine-based drink.\n\n**Wine director –** A role within a group of restaurants or hotels. The person in charge of all wine buying and wine list decisions for a group or chain of restaurants. This role can be combined with that of a ***Head Sommelier*** or often can be an office based role depending on the size of the company. *q.v. **Chef de Vin** .*\n\n**Wine fridge**/**refrigerator** – A fridge specifically designed to store all wines for service. The temperature can be altered to suit sparkling wine to red wine. Some fridges have 2 or more zones so that different types/colours of wine can be stored at different temperatures in the same unit. White wines are generally kept at 6-7*°* Celsius ( 43-44°F) and red wines at 16-18*°* Celsius (62-64°F). *q.v. **Wine cooler***\n\n**Wine glass plate-clip –** A plastic device that clips onto a plate that a stemmed wine glass can slot into and hang safely. This enables the guest at a ***stand-up buffet*** to eat with one hand, while carrying the plate with the other and have a drink, without needing to put anything down.\n\n**Wine station –** Sometimes ***sommelier/somm station***. A service ***station*** where wines can be opened, ***decanted*** and prepared for service by members of the ***wine team***. Generally found in ***fine dining*** restaurants.\n\n**Wine Team** – The group who buy, advise, serve and choose parings of wine for the restaurant. Led by the ***Head Sommelier*** (sometimes called a *wine director* but this can potentially be a role off the floor at group level if the restaurant is part of a larger company) with an ***Assistant Head Sommelier*** and possibly a few ***sommeliers*** and ***commis*** or ***assistant sommeliers***. Other bar staff may come under the Head Sommelier’s management in smaller restaurants.\n\n**Wine waiter/steward –** A rather antiquated term which is not used much any more. Although it can be an outdated term for a ***sommelier***, it more accurately refers to a role that collects wine from the cellar or wine fridge, serves wine and may take the wine order from a table but there is not necessarily any advanced specialist wine knowledge or certifications required as there is for a ***sommelier***. A function which is usually done by a sommelier and/or ***assistant/commis sommelier*** these days.\n\n**Wobble wedge –** A small, plastic, rubber or wooden wedge that gets put under a table leg to prevent it wobbling on uneven floors or surfaces.\n\n### Share this:\n\n* [Share on X (Opens in new window) X](https://thewinechaser.wordpress.com/2022/11/30/restaurant-terms-and-slang/?share=twitter)\n* [Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook](https://thewinechaser.wordpress.com/2022/11/30/restaurant-terms-and-slang/?share=facebook)\n* [Share on Pinterest (Opens in new window) Pinterest](https://thewinechaser.wordpress.com/2022/11/30/restaurant-terms-and-slang/?share=pinterest)\n* [Share on Reddit (Opens in new window) Reddit](https://thewinechaser.wordpress.com/2022/11/30/restaurant-terms-and-slang/?share=reddit)\n* [Share on LinkedIn (Opens in new window) LinkedIn](https://thewinechaser.wordpress.com/2022/11/30/restaurant-terms-and-slang/?share=linkedin)\n\nLike Loading…\n\n### 6 responses to “Restaurant terminology and slang”\n\n1. ![Bar Terminology – The Wine Chaser Avatar](https://thewinechaser.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/cropped-wine-chaser-logo-2.png?w=40)\n\n   [Bar Terminology – The Wine Chaser](https://thewinechaser.wordpress.com/2024/03/27/bar-terminology/)\n\n   […] terms that can confuse anyone starting out in their career. Objectives The fundamental mission of school meal is to meet the physiological and nutritional needs of pupils, helping to maintain their concentration and attention over the whole school day (in primary schools from 8:30 AM to 4 PM; in secondary schools from 8 AM to around 6 PM). The meals served must comply with hygiene and health safety standards, nutrition regulations and information on allergens17. Moreover, the school meal plays a major role in meeting educational, cultural, economic and environmental challenges and establishing social norms around eating. It contributes to developing children's palates and making them aware of balanced eating habits, it teaches them conviviality and introduces them to culinary culture, and it encourages them to prevent waste and protect the environment. School meals must be balanced, varied and age-appropriate in terms of portion sizes. Targeting of lower-income children In primary education, school meals are generally provided by the municipalities and managed by the school fund, which gives its opinion on prices. The financial contribution of families is determined by the municipality. Social pricing can be used, this means that the family's financial contribution varies according to family income (\"quotient familial\"). The “family quotient” is a tool for social equity that makes it possible to calculate the participation families based on their income, family benefits received and the composition of the household. The higher the income is, the higher the price paid by the family. Families experiencing financial difficulties can contact local social workers. This quotient is equal to the household income (monthly taxable income and family benefits, including housing allowance) divided by the number of units in the household (couple or single person = 2 shares; 0.5 share per dependent child; 0.5 additional share for the 3rd child or disabled disabled minor). The application of social pricing by local authorities is encouraged by the government in line with the objective of the national strategy to combat poverty24 which is to guarantee access to food for all. More than 75% of municipalities with more than 10,000 inhabitants apply social pricing. In April 2019, measures were announced to provide access to school canteens at a rate of €1 for the most disadvantaged populations in municipalities with fewer than 10,000 inhabitants. The municipalities concerned receive €3 from the government if the price of meals is less than €125. The meal price of less than or equal to 1€ is awarded to families whose quotient familial is less than or equal to 1,000€ (or equivalent in terms of income depending on the number of children). To reduce the environmental impact and encourage diversification of protein sources, a vegetarian meal (without meat or fish) must be served at least once a week according to the Climate and Resilience Law in 202137. One study of the nutritional quality of about 1,000 main protein dishes actually served in primary schools in France found that both vegetarian category and non-vegetarian dishes displayed adequate levels (≥5% adequacy for 100 kcal) for almost all “protective” nutrients, except for the vegan ones in which key nutrients were lacking (vitamin B12, vitamin D and DHA) or were present in insufficient amounts (vitamin B2 and calcium)38. The carbon footprint of these menus was more than twice as small as that of non-vegetarian menus, confirming that they can contribute to less environmental pressure.
