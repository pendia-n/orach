Trains](https://www.kkday.com/en/blog/wp-content/uploads/Tokyo-Metro-9000_Toei-6300-1024x683.jpg)Tokyo Metro Trains via Wikimedia Commons\n\nThe Tokyo Metro, also known as the Tokyo Subway, is a rapid transit system in greater Tokyo operated by the Tokyo Metro Co. It’s the most convenient and comfortable way to get around Tokyo because of its high frequency of trains and its direct access to tourist spots in the city. Through the Tokyo Metro, you can get access to transportation from Narita Airport and Haneda Airport. You can also easily visit popular places in Tokyo such as the Asakusa Sensoji Temple, Shibuya, Tsukiji Outer Market, and Tokyo Tower!\n\n### **Tokyo Subway Map**\n\n![Tokyo Subway Map](https://www.kkday.com/en/blog/wp-content/uploads/img_01.png)via Tokyo Metro Official Website\n\nTokyo Metro is divided into nine lines: the Ginza Line, Toza Line, Fukutoshi Line, Marunoichi Line, Chiyoda Line, Hanzomon Line, Hibiya Line, Yurakucho Line, and Namboku Line. Each subway line has station numbers. These station numbers are composed of a letter representing the subway line and the number of the station. These station numbers are encircled with a color that represents the subway line so it’s easier for commuters to find their desired station.\n\n### **Tokyo Subway Fares**\n\n![subway tickets](https://www.kkday.com/en/blog/wp-content/uploads/index_img_01-300x111.png)via Tokyo Metro Official Website\n\nYou can buy tickets at vending machines available at every Tokyo Metro station. Tickets are sold in denominations of 180 JPY, 210 JPY, 360 JPY, 260 JPY, 300 JPY, and 330 JPY. Fare prices are based on the distance that you will travel.\n\n## **Buses in Tokyo**\n\n![tokyo toei bus](https://www.kkday.com/en/blog/wp-content/uploads/%E8%B6%B3%E7%AB%8B230%E3%81%82448_%E4%B8%8A23-1024x732.jpg)via Wikimedia Commons\n\nWhile trains and subways are more common, you can also choose to ride buses in Tokyo to get around and visit famous tourist attractions. Buses in Tokyo are run by Toei Transportation and their service extends from central urban areas to Tokyo’s old towns.\n\nTo ride a bus in Tokyo, you must simply go to the nearest bus stop. Bus stops have a timetable of the buses’ schedule including their destination and their estimated time of arrival. You board the bus through the front door, pay your fare, and get off the bus through the rear door.\n\n### **Bus Fares and Bus Passes in Tokyo**\n\nBuses in Tokyo charge a flat fare of 210 JPY for adults and 110 for children. If you are paying your fare in cash, insert your coins in the coin slot. There’s also a bill acceptor in the fare machine that accepts 1,000 JPY bills and automatically gives change.\n\nBus fare machines also accept bus passes. You can use the following:\n\n- PASMO card, an IC card that can be used across Japan for regular bus routes\n- TOEI One-Day Economy Pass. You can use this for day trips around Tokyo — all you have to do is show it to the driver once you board the bus. One-Day Economic Pass is sold at 700 JPY for adults for one-day use and half the price for children.\n\n## Taxis in Tokyo\n\n![](https://www.kkday.com/en/blog/wp-content/uploads/Green_and_yellow_taxi_at_night_in_Yasukuni-dori_Avenue_Shinjuku_Tokyo-1024x768.jpg)via Wikimedia Commons\n\nTaxis in Tokyo are everywhere and fairly easy to catch. They’re a great way to get around the city if you’re carrying a lot of luggage, have children with you, or traveling during midnight when buses and trains have already stopped operating. But fair warning — they’re more expensive than other modes of transportation.\n\nMost taxi ranks can be found outside train stations and bus stations but it’s just as easy to flag a taxi down off a street. You will see a plate on the dashboard on the lower left of the taxi’s windshield — this will help you determine whether the taxi is vacant or not. An illuminated green 賃走 indicates it’s available, an illuminated red 空車 indicates it’s full.\n\nTokyo taxis come in three sizes — the kogatasha 小型車 (small taxi), chuugatasha 中型車 (medium taxi), and ogatasha 大型車 (large taxis). Large and medium taxis in Tokyo can carry up to five passengers, while small taxis can carry up to four.\n\nIn Japan, you enter the taxi on the left side of the vehicle. The driver will usually open and close the door for you. It is recommended that you greet the taxi driver with a polite “Ohayou Gozaimasu” (good morning), “Konnichiwa” (Good afternoon/good day), or “Konbanwa” (good evening) upon boarding. While most taxi drivers can understand basic English, it still helps to write your destination in Japanese on a piece of paper or show them a map of where you’re headed. Tokyo taxis have a satellite navigation system that can be used to search the addresses of your destination.\n\n### **Taxi Fares in Tokyo**\n\nTaxi costs in Tokyo are calculated by the meter. Most taxis charge a flat rate of 730 JPY for the first 1-2 kilometers and increase by 280 JPY for each 280 meters traveled. The fare will continue to increase when you’re stuck in traffic. If the taxi uses expressways, expect toll fees to be added to your fare. Late-night fees are also added when you’re boarding a taxi from 10 PM – 5 AM.\n\nAll taxis accept cash and most taxi operators accept credit cards. Many taxis will have a sticker pasted on the vehicle’s body to indicate what type of payment methods they accept.\n\n## **Tokyo Transport Cards and Passes**\n\n![a hand holding a SUICA card](https://www.kkday.com/en/blog/wp-content/uploads/2656955536_1865e394b1_k-1024x681.jpg)via Marufish on Flickr Creative Commons\n\nTokyo passes are a very convenient and affordable way to get around Tokyo, especially if you’re visiting several tourist attractions throughout the day. You can generally select the language (including English), either insert your IC card or place it on top of the blue reader, and add cash (notes or coins) to recharge. You can also get a receipt if you need it.\n\n### How do I make purchases using an IC card?\n\nImage\n\n![japan-transport-cards](https://travel.rakuten.com/contents/sites/contents/files/styles/max_1300x1300/public/2023-03/japan-transport-cards_4.jpg?itok=y844ZK7p)\n\nAt a range of stores across cities in Japan, you’ll be able to make purchases using an IC card. For example, go to a [fami-restaurant (family restaurant)](https://www.jreast.co.jp/e/suicamoney/shop.html#machinaka-restaurants) or lunch spot in Shinjuku or any convenience store. You can choose to pay with an IC card (just look out for the relevant logos at the register).\n\nThe store person will either ask for your preferred mode of payment, or you might have the option of selecting “IC card” if you’re paying at a self-checkout.\n\n### How do I check the card balance?\n\nYou can generally see this pop up as you tap your card at the ticketing gate. If you’ve still got cash on your card by the end of your trip, you can get back any remaining balance, as well as the 500 JPY you might have paid for a deposit, by visiting a ticketing window of the issuing company. See [this page](https://experiences.travel.rakuten.com/experiences/22500) for information on refunds for a Suica card.\n\n### Is there an expiry date?\n\nShort-term visitor cards are only usable for the allocated period e.g. four weeks, but all other general IC cards will expire 10 years after the last use, so you won’t need to worry about expiry for a while!\n\n### What happens if I forget my IC card?\n\nImage\n\n![japan-transport-cards](https://travel.rakuten.com/contents/sites/contents/files/styles/max_1300x1300/public/2023-03/japan-transport-cards_5.jpg?itok=_KFr0dCe)\n\nImagine you’re already at the train station when you realize you’ve forgotten your IC card… instead of purchasing another one, you can still buy a paper ticket. Just head to the ticketing machine that reads きっぷ (kippu) and select the amount required to get to your destination. To figure out how much cash to insert, you can have a look at the train map (usually above the ticketing machines) which shows the costs to each station on the train line.\n\n[TOP](https://travel.rakuten.com/contents/usa/en-us/guide/japan-transport-cards/#page)",
